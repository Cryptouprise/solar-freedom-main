# Proposed USREA Contributor Draft

## What Homeowners Should Collect Before Relying on a Solar Loan Payment or Sales Claim

*Proposed byline: [Author to be approved]*

Residential solar can be a useful home-energy option, but the paperwork behind a solar purchase can be difficult to interpret. A homeowner may have a solar proposal, an installer agreement, a financing agreement, payment statements, a completion certificate, utility information, and sales messages. These records do not necessarily describe the same thing. The practical first step is not to assume that a confusing number proves a problem. It is to organize the documents so the homeowner can ask the right questions.

The U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has identified areas of consumer risk in some solar financing transactions, including loan markups, tax-credit assumptions, and payment increases connected with expected prepayments. Those findings do not decide any individual borrower’s situation. They do, however, show why a document-first approach matters. A homeowner should be able to see what was represented, what was signed, what was funded, and what is being billed.

### Start With Four Separate Records

The first record is the **signed financing agreement**. It should identify the borrower, lender, principal amount, term, payment schedule, and the terms that govern later payment changes. The second is the **installer agreement**. It typically addresses the equipment, work scope, installation process, and the parties responsible for the project. The third is the **proposal or sales presentation**. It may contain production, savings, tax-credit, financing, or timeline assumptions. The fourth is the **account record**, including current statements, payment history, and any payoff information.

Treating these documents as interchangeable causes confusion. A proposal may use a cash price or an estimate. A financing agreement may show a loan principal and repayment structure. An installer agreement may describe work that is separate from financing terms. Putting the records side by side helps a homeowner identify which question belongs to which party.

### Build a Simple Timeline

A timeline makes the paperwork easier to use. Record the date of the sales conversation, the dates documents were signed, funding date, installation milestones, utility interconnection, and any later service issue. Next to each date, preserve the related email, text message, or document.

This is particularly helpful when a homeowner is trying to understand a payment change, an installation delay, or a statement made during the sale. A written timeline does not make a legal conclusion. It gives a qualified reviewer a factual record and helps the homeowner make specific requests instead of relying on memory.

### Compare the Numbers That Matter

The comparison does not need to be complicated. Homeowners can make a one-page table with these fields:

- Loan principal shown in the financing agreement.
- Term, rate or APR, and payment schedule.
- Any amount or condition tied to a later payment adjustment.
- Cash price or system price shown in the proposal.
- Current balance and payment shown on recent statements.
- Dates and amounts in any funding or completion records.

If two figures differ, the difference is a question to investigate—not an automatic conclusion. The homeowner can ask each company to identify the signed document that explains the number. Requests should be factual and, when possible, made in writing.

### Keep Sales Claims With the Underlying Materials

Sales representations can be hard to reconstruct after a project is underway. Preserve written estimates, advertisements, messages, screenshots, and notes from calls. These items can help distinguish a promise, an illustration, a general statement, or a term actually included in a signed agreement.

The Treasury Department, CFPB, and Federal Trade Commission have published consumer advisories encouraging solar shoppers to compare options carefully and to understand financing before signing. That approach remains useful after a purchase: gather the original materials before deciding what question to raise or what next step to take.

### Ask Precise Questions

An organized file makes it possible to ask better questions. Examples include:

1. Which signed document identifies the payment schedule now shown on my account?
2. Which document explains a later payment increase or re-amortization?
3. Which company is responsible for financing, installation, service, and account support?
4. What record supports a production, savings, tax-credit, or timeline statement in the proposal?
5. Which document explains the relationship between the proposal amount, loan amount, and current balance?

The answers may be straightforward. If they are not, a complete file helps a housing counselor, consumer agency, attorney, or other qualified professional review the facts efficiently.

### Do Not Create a Problem by Acting Too Early

A homeowner who is frustrated by a payment, a delay, or a sales claim may want an immediate answer. But stopping payment, signing a replacement agreement, or relying on a general online claim can have consequences. Financing and installation agreements may involve separate obligations. A document-first review gives the homeowner a sounder basis for an informed decision.

The goal is not to make every solar transaction look suspicious. It is to help consumers use their own records to understand a major household decision. Clear documentation benefits homeowners, lenders, installers, and the broader clean-energy market by making questions easier to answer and misunderstandings easier to address.
